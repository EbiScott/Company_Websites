Lagos builders learn to price the rain.

A generation of developers is redesigning foundations, drainage, and insurance around a wetter city. The maths is finally catching up with the weather.

By Adaeze Okonkwo Lagos 6 min read

On a site in Lekki, the first thing the contractor does is not pour concrete. It is measure the water. The plot sits below the road, and the road sits below the estate, and the estate sits below the kind of rain that arrives in July and refuses to leave.

For years, the answer was a bigger pump and a longer bill. Now a small group of builders is treating drainage as part of the design, not a problem to solve after the client moves in. The change is expensive up front and cheaper over the life of the building, which is exactly the argument the industry has historically ignored.

“You cannot sell a foundation people cannot see,” one engineer said. “So we stopped selling it. We started showing the flood map.”

“A city that cannot drain itself is a city that cannot plan.”

By the editorial board

The temptation is to treat every flood as an act of God. It is more useful to treat it as an act of procurement. The drains exist or they do not. The permits were checked or they were not. The maintenance budget was spent or it was moved somewhere else.

Lagos does not need a new story about the rain. It needs a boring, annual, published account of what was built and what was fixed. We would read it. We suspect you would too.
